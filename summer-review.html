Naming becomes much easier when the first step is always classify the compound. Ionic compounds, molecular compounds, acids, and hydrates use different rules.

Wrong classification = wrong naming system. Do not use prefixes for ordinary ionic compounds, and do not use Roman numerals for molecular compounds.
Binary ionic compounds

Write the cation first and the anion second. A monatomic anion changes its ending to -ide.

NaCl → sodium chloride
Mg₃N₂ → magnesium nitride

Name → formula

1write ion charges

Mg²⁺ and N³⁻

2balance charge

total + = total −

3use lowest ratio

Mg₃N₂

Why must the charges balance? The written compound is electrically neutral overall.
Roman numerals for variable-charge metals

The Roman numeral tells the charge on one metal ion, not the number of atoms.

CuO

Oxide is O²⁻. One Cu balances one O²⁻, so Cu must be +2.

copper(II) oxide
Cu₂O

O contributes −2 total. Two Cu atoms must contribute +2 total, so each is +1.

copper(I) oxide
Ni₃(PO₄)₂

Two PO₄³⁻ ions contribute −6. Three Ni must total +6, so each Ni is +2.

nickel(II) phosphate
Polyatomic-ion compounds and parentheses

Treat a polyatomic ion as a single charged unit. If more than one copy is required, use parentheses.

Al³⁺ + SO₄²⁻ → Al₂(SO₄)₃
Zn²⁺ + HSO₄⁻ → Zn(HSO₄)₂
Do not alter the inside of a polyatomic ion. Sulfate stays SO₄²⁻; balancing charge changes the number of sulfate ions, not sulfate's internal formula.

II · Dimensional Analysis — Let the Units Tell You What to Do

Dimensional analysis is not a separate chemistry trick. It is a general method for multiplying by forms of “1” until the unwanted units disappear.

What a conversion factor actually is

If two quantities are equivalent, their ratio equals 1.

1 in = 2.54 cm
2.54 cm / 1 in = 1    and    1 in / 2.54 cm = 1

Multiplying by either ratio does not change the physical quantity; it only changes how the quantity is expressed.

The unit-cancellation method
1write given

include its unit

2choose target

what unit must remain?

3build factors

unwanted units cancel diagonally

4calculate

only after units are correct

26 in → cm
26 in × (2.54 cm / 1 in) = 66 cm
If the unwanted unit does not cancel, the factor is upside down.
Multi-step conversions
30.0 m → miles using yards
30.0 m × (1.094 yd/1 m) × (1 mi/1760 yd)

Meters cancel, then yards cancel, leaving miles.

The power of this method is that you do not need one direct conversion. You can build a path.

Squared and cubed units

A conversion factor must be raised to the same power as the unit.

1 m = 100 cm
1 m² = (100 cm)² = 10,000 cm²
1 m³ = (100 cm)³ = 1,000,000 cm³
Classic mistake: converting 1 m² as 100 cm². Length was squared, so the factor must be squared too.
Density as a conversion factor
D = m/V

A density of 2.33 g/cm³ means:

2.33 g / 1 cm³

It can convert mass ↔ volume exactly like any other factor.

2170 g Si with density 2.33 g/cm³
V = 2170 g × (1 cm³/2.33 g) ≈ 931 cm³
Temperature is the major exception

Temperature scales use an offset, so Celsius/Kelvin conversion is not done by a simple factor.

K = °C + 273.15
Why dimensional analysis does not work here: 0°C is not 0 K, so the scales are not related by a simple multiplicative ratio.
Answer-quality checks
  • Write units at every step.
  • Check whether the numerical size should increase or decrease.
  • Do not round early in a multi-step problem.
  • Apply significant figures after the conversion path is correct.
III · Significant Figures — What the Digits Say About Measurement

Significant figures communicate measurement precision. They are not arbitrary formatting rules: the last reported digit is the estimated/uncertain digit.

Measured vs exact numbers

A measured quantity is limited by the measuring instrument. A counted or defined quantity can be exact.

Measured

12.4 mL from glassware → finite precision

Exact

3 molecules or 1 in = 2.54 cm by definition → unlimited sig figs

Counting significant figures
RuleExampleSig figs
nonzero digits count3573
leading zeros do not count0.00422
captive zeros count100235
trailing decimal zeros count4.7004
trailing zeros without a decimal can be ambiguous1000notation-dependent
Scientific notation removes ambiguity: 1.00×10³ clearly has 3 significant figures.
Multiplication and division

The answer is limited by the input with the fewest significant figures.

1.827 m × 0.762 m
calculator: 1.392174 m²

0.762 has 3 sig figs → report 1.39 m².

Addition and subtraction

The answer is limited by the least precise decimal place, not total sig-fig count.

49.146 m + 72.13 m
121.276 m → 121.28 m

The least precise measurement is to the hundredths place.

Mixed calculations and guard digits

Keep extra calculator digits during intermediate steps and round once at the end, unless the problem specifically requires otherwise.

Why? Rounding intermediate values can push the final answer away from the value obtained from the original data.
Accuracy vs precision
AccuracyPrecision
closeness to accepted/true valuecloseness of repeated measurements to one another
% error = |experimental − accepted| / accepted ×100

A set can be precise but inaccurate if repeated values cluster tightly around the wrong value.

IV · Scientific Notation — Control Very Large and Very Small Numbers
Structure and decimal movement
a × 10ⁿ where 1 ≤ |a| < 10
350,000,000 = 3.5 × 10⁸
0.0000721 = 7.21 × 10⁻⁵
Exponent sign check: large ordinary numbers need positive powers; tiny positive numbers need negative powers.
Multiplication and division with powers of ten
(a×10ᵐ)(b×10ⁿ) = ab×10ᵐ⁺ⁿ
(a×10ᵐ)/(b×10ⁿ) = (a/b)×10ᵐ⁻ⁿ

After calculating, renormalize the coefficient so it lies between 1 and 10.

Addition and subtraction

Exponents must match before coefficients can be added or subtracted.

3.2×10⁵ + 4.7×10⁴ = 3.2×10⁵ + 0.47×10⁵ = 3.67×10⁵
Calculator entry

Use the calculator's EE/EXP key for “×10^”. Entering 6.022 EXP 23 avoids extra parentheses and common order-of-operations mistakes.

Do not type ×10^ twice. On many calculators, EXP already means “times ten to the power.”
V · Chemical Reactions — Predict, Classify, Balance, and Explain

A chemical equation is a particle-accounting statement. The formulas tell what substances are present; coefficients tell how many particles or moles react.

What a chemical equation communicates
reactants → products

State symbols: (s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas, (aq) dissolved in water.

Balanced equations conserve atoms. The law of conservation of mass requires the same number of each kind of atom before and after a reaction.
Never change subscripts to balance. A subscript changes substance identity. Change coefficients only.
How to balance systematically
  1. Write correct formulas first.
  2. Count each element on both sides.
  3. Adjust coefficients one substance at a time.
  4. Leave H and O until later when convenient.
  5. Reduce coefficients to the lowest whole-number ratio.
Propane combustion
C₃H₈ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O

Balance C: 3CO₂. Balance H: 4H₂O. Product side now has 10 O atoms → 5O₂.

C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O

Reaction categories can overlap. Combustion is also a redox process, for example.

Diatomic elemental forms
H₂ N₂ O₂ F₂ Cl₂ Br₂ I₂

When these elements appear in their free elemental state, use the diatomic formula. This matters when predicting products.

Single replacement and activity

A single-replacement reaction occurs only if the free element can displace the corresponding element/ion in the compound. An activity series or supplied chemical knowledge determines whether the reaction occurs.

Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu
Pattern alone is not enough. Some apparent single-replacement setups are “no reaction.”
Double replacement, solubility, and precipitates

Swap ions, balance charge in each new formula, then apply solubility rules. An insoluble product is a precipitate.

AgNO₃ + KCl
AgNO₃(aq)+KCl(aq)→AgCl(s)+KNO₃(aq)

AgCl is insoluble, so precipitation drives the reaction.

Acid-base neutralization

A strong acid and strong base react to produce water and a salt.

H⁺(aq)+OH⁻(aq)→H₂O(l)

If a weak acid/base participates, it usually remains molecular in a net ionic equation.

Redox basics
oxidation = loss of electrons
reduction = gain of electrons

Oxidation and reduction always occur together because electrons lost by one species must be gained by another.

VIII · Bonding & Lewis Structures — Build Molecules Electron by Electron

Lewis structures are bookkeeping models for valence electrons. They help predict connectivity, lone pairs, multiple bonds, formal charges, resonance, and eventually molecular shape.

Valence electrons and the octet idea

Main-group atoms often form bonds that give them noble-gas-like valence arrangements. Hydrogen seeks a duet; many second-row elements are most stable with eight valence electrons.

But “octet rule” is a useful model, not a universal law. BF₃ can have an incomplete octet on B; third-period and heavier elements can appear with expanded valence counts in common Lewis representations.
Count total valence electrons first
  1. Add valence electrons from every atom.
  2. Add one electron for each negative charge.
  3. Subtract one electron for each positive charge.
NO₃⁻
N:5 + 3(O:6) +1 charge electron =24 valence e⁻

Formal charge
formal charge = valence e⁻ − nonbonding e⁻ − number of bond lines

Formal charge helps compare valid Lewis structures. Favor structures with smaller charge magnitudes when other rules are comparable, and place negative formal charge on more electronegative atoms when possible.

Resonance

Sometimes more than one valid Lewis structure differs only in electron placement. These are resonance contributors; the real electron distribution is not flipping between drawings but is delocalized.

Nitrate, NO₃⁻

Three equivalent contributors can be drawn with the N=O double bond in three positions. The actual N–O bonds are equivalent in the resonance hybrid.

Sulfate, SO₄²⁻

Common AP Lewis representations use resonance and formal-charge reasoning; follow the class convention for expanded-octet sulfur representations.

Ionic vs covalent bonding connection

Ionic: attraction among oppositely charged ions in an extended lattice; formula gives simplest ion ratio.

Covalent: electron pairs are shared between atoms; discrete molecules are common.

Na + Cl → Na⁺ + Cl⁻ → ionic lattice
H + H → H—H → covalent molecule
IX · Stoichiometry — Use a Balanced Equation as a Chemical Recipe

Stoichiometry is dimensional analysis with a balanced chemical equation inserted between the known substance and the desired substance.

What coefficients mean
N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃

This means 1 molecule N₂ reacts with 3 molecules H₂, and therefore 1 mol N₂ reacts with 3 mol H₂ to form 2 mol NH₃.

Coefficients are mole ratios. They are not mass ratios. Never use 1 g N₂ : 3 g H₂ simply because the coefficients are 1:3.
Universal stoichiometry roadmap
given unitconvert to mol
→
mol knownbalanced-equation ratio
→
mol wantedconvert to requested unit

If grams are given and grams are requested, the full chain is:

g A → mol A → mol B → g B
Mass-to-mass example

For propane combustion:

C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O

Every 1 mol propane produces 4 mol water. A mass problem converts propane mass → mol propane → mol water → mass water.

Limiting reactant — the ingredient that runs out

When amounts of two reactants are supplied, do not choose the one with fewer grams or fewer moles automatically. Compare amounts using the balanced equation.

1balance equation
2convert both to moles
3calculate product from each
4smaller product wins

that reactant is limiting

Al + Cl₂ example from the stoichiometry notes
2Al + 3Cl₂ → 2AlCl₃

10.0 g Al could make about 49.4 g AlCl₃; 35.0 g Cl₂ could make about 43.9 g AlCl₃.

Cl₂ is limiting because it produces less product.
Excess reactant remaining

Once the limiting reactant is identified, start with the limiting reactant and calculate how much excess reactant was actually consumed. Then subtract from the amount initially present.

excess remaining = excess initial − excess used

In the Al/Cl₂ class example, the notes calculate about 8.8 g Al used from 10.0 g, leaving about 1.2 g Al.

Theoretical yield, actual yield, percent yield
TermMeaning
theoretical yieldmaximum product predicted from limiting reactant
actual yieldproduct actually isolated experimentally
% yield = actual yield / theoretical yield ×100

Actual yield can be lower because of incomplete reactions, side reactions, loss during transfer/purification, or measurement limitations.

Particle-diagram stoichiometry

Treat each pictured molecule as a countable particle and group particles according to coefficients. For N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃, every set of 1 N₂ and 3 H₂ makes 2 NH₃. The reagent that cannot form another complete set is limiting.

Empirical-formula stoichiometry connection

Combustion analysis is another stoichiometric chain. CO₂ tells you the amount of carbon; H₂O tells you the amount of hydrogen; oxygen can be found by mass difference when appropriate. Convert recovered elemental amounts to moles and simplify the ratio.

Hydrate calculations

Heating a hydrate drives off water. Use the mass lost to find moles H₂O and the remaining anhydrous salt mass to find moles salt.

hydrate mass − anhydrous mass = mass H₂O
moles H₂O / moles salt → hydrate subscript
General pattern

If the ratio is 1 mol salt : 5 mol H₂O, the hydrate is salt·5H₂O and is named pentahydrate.

Stoichiometry error checklist
  • Equation must be balanced before using coefficients.
  • Coefficients compare moles, not grams.
  • Do not use the excess reactant to calculate final product after finding the limiting reactant.
  • The limiting reactant is not necessarily the smallest mass or smallest number of moles.
  • Track units through every conversion.
  • Round at the end and apply sig figs.
