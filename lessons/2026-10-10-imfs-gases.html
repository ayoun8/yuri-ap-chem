AP Chemistry 3.1 · Key lesson content

Intermolecular Forces: Explained & Applied

Intermolecular forces (IMFs) act between separate particles. They are distinct from intramolecular bonds, which hold the atoms within a molecule together. Stronger attractions generally make it harder to separate molecules, often increasing boiling point and viscosity for otherwise comparable substances.

InteractionWhen it occursHow to recognize it
London dispersion (LDF)All atoms and moleculesTemporary induced dipoles; generally stronger with greater polarizability/electron count and more exposed molecular surface area.
Dipole–dipolePolar moleculesAttraction between permanent partial positive and negative regions. Molecular geometry matters: polar bonds do not always mean a polar molecule.
Hydrogen bondingSuitable molecules with H directly bonded to N, O, or F and an available lone-pair acceptorA particularly strong directional dipole–dipole attraction. NH₃ and H₂O can hydrogen-bond; CH₄ cannot hydrogen-bond with itself.
Ion–dipoleIons interacting with polar moleculesImportant when aqueous solutions hydrate dissolved ions, such as Na⁺ surrounded by water.
Decision routine: (1) Identify whether the substance consists of ions or molecules. (2) For molecules, check molecular geometry and net polarity. (3) Check for hydrogen-bond donors/acceptors. (4) Compare polarizability and surface area—especially when molecules are similar. Every molecule has LDFs, even if it also has other IMFs.

Why the AP Classroom questions required IMF reasoning

Ar vs. Ne: Ar has more electrons and is more polarizable, so its dispersion attractions are stronger. At extremely high pressure, Ar's larger finite particle volume is the most direct explanation for the size-related deviation in the reviewed question.
NH₃ vs. CH₄: NH₃ is polar and can hydrogen-bond; CH₄ is nonpolar and relies on LDFs. Attractions among NH₃ molecules reduce its measured pressure relative to an ideal prediction under comparable conditions.
Compressing Xe: Increasing the number of atoms in the same space brings particles closer together, making nonideal effects more important.

New worked example · Boiling-point comparison

Why does 1-butanol boil at a much higher temperature than pentane?

Step 1 — Compare the substances: Pentane (C₅H₁₂) and 1-butanol (C₄H₉OH) have similar molar masses, making intermolecular attractions a useful focus.

Step 2 — Identify IMFs: Pentane is nonpolar and exhibits LDFs. 1-butanol has LDFs and dipole–dipole interactions, and its O–H groups can form intermolecular hydrogen bonds.

Step 3 — Connect to the property: Separating 1-butanol molecules requires overcoming stronger overall intermolecular attractions; therefore, its boiling point is much higher.

Trap: Boiling does not break the molecules' covalent C–O or O–H bonds. It separates molecules by overcoming attractions between them.

New worked example · Molecular shape and dispersion

Pentane versus 2-methylbutane: Which has the higher boiling point?

Step 1: Both are nonpolar C₅H₁₂ isomers; both depend primarily on LDFs.

Step 2: Straight-chain pentane has a more extended shape with greater effective contact area than the branched isomer.

Step 3: Greater contact area strengthens dispersion attractions, so pentane generally has the higher boiling point.

Trap: They have the same number of electrons and the same molar mass; the key variable here is shape and surface area.

Extra IMF practice

Practice A · H₂O vs. H₂S: Predict the higher boiling point and explain.
Answer: H₂O. Water molecules hydrogen-bond strongly because H is directly bound to O; H₂S lacks comparably strong hydrogen bonding. The stronger attractions in H₂O require more energy to overcome. Do not justify this solely by molecular mass: H₂S is heavier.
Practice B · Which interaction is present between Na⁺ and a nearby water molecule?
Answer: Ion–dipole. The positive ion attracts the partially negative oxygen end of H₂O. This differs from hydrogen bonding between water molecules.
Practice C · Why can I₂ show stronger LDFs than F₂?
Answer: I₂ has more electrons in a larger, more polarizable electron cloud, producing stronger instantaneous/induced-dipole attractions. Both are nonpolar diatomic molecules.

Worked examples — show every step

Original practice problems targeting the reasoning patterns covered during the session—not copied AP Classroom questions. Complete each problem independently, then expand the solution.

Example 1 · Select the gas law: compression at constant temperature

Problem: A syringe traps 3.60 L of gas at 0.95 atm. The plunger reduces the volume to 1.80 L without changing temperature. Find the final pressure.

1. Identify constants: temperature and gas amount remain constant, so apply Boyle’s law: P₁V₁ = P₂V₂.
2. Solve symbolically: P₂ = P₁V₁/V₂.
3. Substitute: P₂ = (0.95 atm)(3.60 L)/(1.80 L) = 1.9 atm.
4. Sanity check: volume halves, so pressure should double.

Teaching point: When the scenario says a piston moves and T is constant, think inverse P–V relationship. Don’t apply a law before identifying what stays fixed.

Example 2 · Convert Celsius to Kelvin before calculating

Problem: A balloon occupies 2.40 L at 27 °C. At constant pressure, what volume will it have at 77 °C?

1. Choose Charles’s law: V₁/T₁ = V₂/T₂ because pressure and moles are fixed.
2. Convert: T₁ = 27 + 273.15 = 300.15 K and T₂ = 77 + 273.15 = 350.15 K.
3. Rearrange: V₂ = 2.40 × 350.15/300.15 ≈ 2.80 L.
4. Check direction: hotter at fixed pressure means larger volume.

Common trap: Using 77/27 instead of Kelvin gives a wildly inflated answer.

Example 3 · Ideal gas equation with explicit units

Problem: What is the pressure of 0.250 mol gas in a 5.00 L vessel at 300 K?

1. Identify variables: n = 0.250 mol, V = 5.00 L, T = 300 K.
2. Use PV = nRT: P = nRT/V, with R = 0.08206 L·atm·mol⁻¹·K⁻¹.
3. Calculate: P = (0.250)(0.08206)(300)/5.00 = 1.23 atm.
4. Units: mol and K cancel; L cancels, leaving atm.

Teaching point: The units of R must match the pressure and volume units provided.

Example 4 · Mole fractions, Dalton's law and total pressure

Problem: A 1.80-atm gas mixture has 0.20 mol He, 0.30 mol O₂, and 0.50 mol N₂. Find each partial pressure.

1. Total moles: n(total) = 0.20 + 0.30 + 0.50 = 1.00 mol.
2. Mole fractions: X(He)=0.20, X(O₂)=0.30, X(N₂)=0.50.
3. Partial pressures: P(He)=0.36 atm; P(O₂)=0.54 atm; P(N₂)=0.90 atm.
4. Verify: 0.36 + 0.54 + 0.90 = 1.80 atm.

Common trap: The number of moles is not itself a pressure. First calculate the mole fraction, then multiply by total pressure.

Example 5 · Mixing gases after removing a barrier

Problem: Gas A fills 2.0 L at 1.20 atm; gas B fills a separate 2.0 L section at 0.80 atm. A divider is removed, allowing both gases to occupy the full 4.0 L. Temperature stays constant. Find final partial and total pressures.

1. New volume for each gas: each expands from 2.0 L to 4.0 L.
2. Apply Boyle's law separately: P(A) = 1.20 × (2.0/4.0) = 0.60 atm; P(B) = 0.80 × (2.0/4.0) = 0.40 atm.
3. Add partial pressures: P(total) = 0.60 + 0.40 = 1.00 atm.
4. Critical distinction: The sum of the initial compartment pressures is 2.00 atm, but those pressures referred to two different halves; it is not the initial pressure of a homogeneous mixture occupying 4.0 L.

Teaching point: If a question asks whether a gas's pressure halves, first ask whether that particular gas's accessible volume doubled.

Example 6 · Gas collected over water

Problem: A gas is collected above water at a total pressure of 755 torr. Water vapor contributes 24 torr. Find the dry gas pressure.

1. Identify components: P(total) = P(dry gas) + P(water vapor).
2. Rearrange: P(dry gas) = P(total) − P(water).
3. Substitute: 755 − 24 = 731 torr.

Trap: Subtract water vapor pressure rather than treating the total measured pressure as the dry gas pressure. This is the setup in Dingle page 28–29.

Example 7 · Compare RMS speeds and graph shapes

Problem: Compare the root-mean-square speeds of He (4 g/mol) and Ne (20 g/mol) at the same temperature. Which distribution lies farther right?

1. Same temperature: average translational kinetic energy is identical for both gases.
2. Use proportionality: u(rms) ∝ 1/√M at fixed temperature.
3. Speed ratio: u(He)/u(Ne) = √(20/4) = √5 ≈ 2.24.
4. Interpret graph: He's speed curve lies farther right and is broader/lower; Ne is left-shifted and narrower/taller for normalized distributions.

Common trap: Same kinetic energy does not mean same molecular speed. Lighter particles need higher speeds for the same average kinetic energy.
