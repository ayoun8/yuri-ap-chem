Empirical vs Molecular Formula — Complete Guide

Know cold

Empirical formula = simplest whole-number ratio of atoms. Molecular formula = actual number of each atom in one molecule and is always a whole-number multiple of the empirical formula.

Empirical formula

Answers: “What is the simplest ratio?”

C₆H₁₂O₆ → CH₂O

Molecular formula

Answers: “How many atoms are actually in the molecule?”

CH₂O × 6 → C₆H₁₂O₆
Method A: Given masses of each element
Convert every element from grams to moles.
Never compare gram amounts directly. Atomic masses differ.
Divide every mole amount by the smallest mole amount.
This normalizes one element to 1.
Inspect the ratios.
If they are close to whole numbers, use them as subscripts. If not, multiply all ratios by the same small integer.
Write the empirical formula.
Ratio after dividingWhat to do
1.00, 2.00, 3.00…Use directly.
1.50Multiply all ratios by 2.
1.33 or 2.67Multiply all by 3.
1.25 or 1.75Multiply all by 4.
1.20, 1.40, 1.60, 1.80Often multiply all by 5.
Do not round 1.50 to 2. A ratio of 1 : 1.5 becomes 2 : 3, not 1 : 2.
Method B: Given percentages
Assume 100.0 g of compound.
Then each percentage becomes the same number of grams: 40.0% C → 40.0 g C.
Convert grams to moles for every element.
Divide by the smallest mole amount.
Make the ratio whole-numbered and write the empirical formula.
How to get the molecular formula
Find the empirical formula first.
Calculate the empirical-formula mass.
Find the multiplier n.
n = molar mass of compound ÷ empirical-formula mass
Round n to the nearest whole number only if the chemistry clearly supports it.
Multiply every empirical subscript by n.
Summer packet example: Fe and O

Given: 11.66 g Fe, 5.01 g O; molar mass = 320 g/mol.

Fe: 11.66 ÷ 55.845 = 0.2088 mol
O: 5.01 ÷ 15.999 = 0.3131 mol
Divide by 0.2088 → Fe : O = 1.000 : 1.500

Multiply both by 2:

2 : 3 → empirical formula Fe₂O₃
empirical mass ≈ 159.69 g/mol
320 ÷ 159.69 ≈ 2
Packet worksheet method: empirical = Fe₂O₃; molecular-form calculation = Fe₄O₆.
Chemistry convention: ionic compounds are normally written as simplest-ratio formula units, so Fe₂O₃ is the standard chemical formula. The packet is using this item to practice the empirical/molecular calculation procedure.
Summer packet example: Sn and F
Sn: 5.28 ÷ 118.71 = 0.04448 mol
F: 3.37 ÷ 18.998 = 0.1774 mol
Divide by 0.04448 → 1 : 3.99 ≈ 1 : 4
Empirical formula = SnF₄
empirical mass ≈ 194.70 g/mol; 584.1 ÷ 194.70 = 3
Packet worksheet molecular-form calculation = Sn₃F₁₂.
Fast error check
  • If you used grams as subscripts, restart: convert to moles first.
  • If you have decimal subscripts, you are not finished.
  • If your “molecular formula” is not an integer multiple of the empirical formula, something is wrong.
  • If n is nowhere near an integer, recheck molar masses and rounding.
  • Do not simplify the final molecular formula after multiplying; simplification would turn it back into an empirical formula.

Binary ionic compounds

Write the cation first and the anion second. A monatomic anion changes its ending to -ide.

Roman numerals for variable-charge metals

The Roman numeral tells the charge on one metal ion, not the number of atoms.

CuO

Oxide is O²⁻. One Cu balances one O²⁻, so Cu must be +2.

copper(II) oxide

Polyatomic-ion compounds and parentheses

II · Dimensional Analysis — Let the Units Tell You What to Do

Dimensional analysis is not a separate chemistry trick. It is a general method for multiplying by forms of “1” until the unwanted units disappear.

What a conversion factor actually is

If two quantities are equivalent, their ratio equals 1.

1 in = 2.54 cm
2.54 cm / 1 in = 1    and    1 in / 2.54 cm = 1

Multiplying by either ratio does not change the physical quantity; it only changes how the quantity is expressed.

The unit-cancellation method
1write given

include its unit

2choose target

what unit must remain?

3build factors

unwanted units cancel diagonally

4calculate

only after units are correct

26 in → cm
26 in × (2.54 cm / 1 in) = 66 cm
If the unwanted unit does not cancel, the factor is upside down.
Multi-step conversions
30.0 m → miles using yards
30.0 m × (1.094 yd/1 m) × (1 mi/1760 yd)

Meters cancel, then yards cancel, leaving miles.

The power of this method is that you do not need one direct conversion. You can build a path.

Squared and cubed units

A conversion factor must be raised to the same power as the unit.

1 m = 100 cm
1 m² = (100 cm)² = 10,000 cm²
1 m³ = (100 cm)³ = 1,000,000 cm³
Classic mistake: converting 1 m² as 100 cm². Length was squared, so the factor must be squared too.
Density as a conversion factor
D = m/V

A density of 2.33 g/cm³ means:

2.33 g / 1 cm³

It can convert mass ↔ volume exactly like any other factor.

2170 g Si with density 2.33 g/cm³
V = 2170 g × (1 cm³/2.33 g) ≈ 931 cm³
Temperature is the major exception

Temperature scales use an offset, so Celsius/Kelvin conversion is not done by a simple factor.

K = °C + 273.15
Why dimensional analysis does not work here: 0°C is not 0 K, so the scales are not related by a simple multiplicative ratio.
Answer-quality checks
  • Write units at every step.
  • Check whether the numerical size should increase or decrease.
  • Do not round early in a multi-step problem.
  • Apply significant figures after the conversion path is correct.
III · Significant Figures — What the Digits Say About Measurement

Significant figures communicate measurement precision. They are not arbitrary formatting rules: the last reported digit is the estimated/uncertain digit.

Measured vs exact numbers

A measured quantity is limited by the measuring instrument. A counted or defined quantity can be exact.

Measured

12.4 mL from glassware → finite precision

Exact

3 molecules or 1 in = 2.54 cm by definition → unlimited sig figs

Counting significant figures
RuleExampleSig figs
nonzero digits count3573
leading zeros do not count0.00422
captive zeros count100235
trailing decimal zeros count4.7004
trailing zeros without a decimal can be ambiguous1000notation-dependent
Scientific notation removes ambiguity: 1.00×10³ clearly has 3 significant figures.
Multiplication and division

The answer is limited by the input with the fewest significant figures.

1.827 m × 0.762 m
calculator: 1.392174 m²

0.762 has 3 sig figs → report 1.39 m².

Addition and subtraction

The answer is limited by the least precise decimal place, not total sig-fig count.

49.146 m + 72.13 m
121.276 m → 121.28 m

The least precise measurement is to the hundredths place.

Mixed calculations and guard digits

Keep extra calculator digits during intermediate steps and round once at the end, unless the problem specifically requires otherwise.

Why? Rounding intermediate values can push the final answer away from the value obtained from the original data.
Accuracy vs precision
AccuracyPrecision
closeness to accepted/true valuecloseness of repeated measurements to one another
% error = |experimental − accepted| / accepted ×100

A set can be precise but inaccurate if repeated values cluster tightly around the wrong value.

IV · Scientific Notation — Control Very Large and Very Small Numbers
Structure and decimal movement
a × 10ⁿ where 1 ≤ |a| < 10
350,000,000 = 3.5 × 10⁸
0.0000721 = 7.21 × 10⁻⁵
Exponent sign check: large ordinary numbers need positive powers; tiny positive numbers need negative powers.
Multiplication and division with powers of ten
(a×10ᵐ)(b×10ⁿ) = ab×10ᵐ⁺ⁿ
(a×10ᵐ)/(b×10ⁿ) = (a/b)×10ᵐ⁻ⁿ

After calculating, renormalize the coefficient so it lies between 1 and 10.

Addition and subtraction

Exponents must match before coefficients can be added or subtracted.

3.2×10⁵ + 4.7×10⁴ = 3.2×10⁵ + 0.47×10⁵ = 3.67×10⁵
Calculator entry

Use the calculator's EE/EXP key for “×10^”. Entering 6.022 EXP 23 avoids extra parentheses and common order-of-operations mistakes.

Do not type ×10^ twice. On many calculators, EXP already means “times ten to the power.”
V · Chemical Reactions — Predict, Classify, Balance, and Explain

A chemical equation is a particle-accounting statement. The formulas tell what substances are present; coefficients tell how many particles or moles react.

What a chemical equation communicates
reactants → products

State symbols: (s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas, (aq) dissolved in water.

Balanced equations conserve atoms. The law of conservation of mass requires the same number of each kind of atom before and after a reaction.
Never change subscripts to balance. A subscript changes substance identity. Change coefficients only.
How to balance systematically
  1. Write correct formulas first.
  2. Count each element on both sides.
  3. Adjust coefficients one substance at a time.
  4. Leave H and O until later when convenient.
  5. Reduce coefficients to the lowest whole-number ratio.
Propane combustion
C₃H₈ + O₂ → CO₂ + H₂O

Balance C: 3CO₂. Balance H: 4H₂O. Product side now has 10 O atoms → 5O₂.

C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O

Diatomic elemental forms
H₂ N₂ O₂ F₂ Cl₂ Br₂ I₂

When these elements appear in their free elemental state, use the diatomic formula. This matters when predicting products.

Single replacement and activity

A single-replacement reaction occurs only if the free element can displace the corresponding element/ion in the compound. An activity series or supplied chemical knowledge determines whether the reaction occurs.

Zn + CuSO₄ → ZnSO₄ + Cu
Pattern alone is not enough. Some apparent single-replacement setups are “no reaction.”
Double replacement, solubility, and precipitates

Swap ions, balance charge in each new formula, then apply solubility rules. An insoluble product is a precipitate.

AgNO₃ + KCl
AgNO₃(aq)+KCl(aq)→AgCl(s)+KNO₃(aq)

AgCl is insoluble, so precipitation drives the reaction.

A strong acid and strong base react to produce water and a salt.

VIII · Bonding & Lewis Structures — Build Molecules Electron by Electron

Lewis structures are bookkeeping models for valence electrons. They help predict connectivity, lone pairs, multiple bonds, formal charges, resonance, and eventually molecular shape.

Valence electrons and the octet idea

Main-group atoms often form bonds that give them noble-gas-like valence arrangements. Hydrogen seeks a duet; many second-row elements are most stable with eight valence electrons.

But “octet rule” is a useful model, not a universal law. BF₃ can have an incomplete octet on B; third-period and heavier elements can appear with expanded valence counts in common Lewis representations.
Count total valence electrons first
  1. Add valence electrons from every atom.
  2. Add one electron for each negative charge.
  3. Subtract one electron for each positive charge.

Formal charge
formal charge = valence e⁻ − nonbonding e⁻ − number of bond lines

Formal charge helps compare valid Lewis structures. Favor structures with smaller charge magnitudes when other rules are comparable, and place negative formal charge on more electronegative atoms when possible.

Sometimes more than one valid Lewis structure differs only in electron placement. These are resonance contributors; the real electron distribution is not flipping between drawings but is delocalized.

Ionic vs covalent bonding connection

Ionic: attraction among oppositely charged ions in an extended lattice; formula gives simplest ion ratio.

Covalent: electron pairs are shared between atoms; discrete molecules are common.

Na + Cl → Na⁺ + Cl⁻ → ionic lattice
H + H → H—H → covalent molecule
IX · Stoichiometry — Use a Balanced Equation as a Chemical Recipe

Stoichiometry is dimensional analysis with a balanced chemical equation inserted between the known substance and the desired substance.

What coefficients mean
N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃

This means 1 molecule N₂ reacts with 3 molecules H₂, and therefore 1 mol N₂ reacts with 3 mol H₂ to form 2 mol NH₃.

Coefficients are mole ratios. They are not mass ratios. Never use 1 g N₂ : 3 g H₂ simply because the coefficients are 1:3.

If grams are given and grams are requested, the full chain is:

g A → mol A → mol B → g B
Mass-to-mass example

For propane combustion:

C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O

Every 1 mol propane produces 4 mol water. A mass problem converts propane mass → mol propane → mol water → mass water.

Limiting reactant — the ingredient that runs out

When amounts of two reactants are supplied, do not choose the one with fewer grams or fewer moles automatically. Compare amounts using the balanced equation.

1balance equation
2convert both to moles
3calculate product from each
4smaller product wins

that reactant is limiting

Al + Cl₂ example from the stoichiometry notes
2Al + 3Cl₂ → 2AlCl₃

10.0 g Al could make about 49.4 g AlCl₃; 35.0 g Cl₂ could make about 43.9 g AlCl₃.

Cl₂ is limiting because it produces less product.
Excess reactant remaining

Once the limiting reactant is identified, start with the limiting reactant and calculate how much excess reactant was actually consumed. Then subtract from the amount initially present.

excess remaining = excess initial − excess used

In the Al/Cl₂ class example, the notes calculate about 8.8 g Al used from 10.0 g, leaving about 1.2 g Al.

Theoretical yield, actual yield, percent yield
TermMeaning
theoretical yieldmaximum product predicted from limiting reactant
actual yieldproduct actually isolated experimentally
% yield = actual yield / theoretical yield ×100

Actual yield can be lower because of incomplete reactions, side reactions, loss during transfer/purification, or measurement limitations.

Particle-diagram stoichiometry

Treat each pictured molecule as a countable particle and group particles according to coefficients. For N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃, every set of 1 N₂ and 3 H₂ makes 2 NH₃. The reagent that cannot form another complete set is limiting.

Empirical-formula stoichiometry connection

Combustion analysis is another stoichiometric chain. CO₂ tells you the amount of carbon; H₂O tells you the amount of hydrogen; oxygen can be found by mass difference when appropriate. Convert recovered elemental amounts to moles and simplify the ratio.

Hydrate calculations

Heating a hydrate drives off water. Use the mass lost to find moles H₂O and the remaining anhydrous salt mass to find moles salt.

hydrate mass − anhydrous mass = mass H₂O
moles H₂O / moles salt → hydrate subscript
General pattern

If the ratio is 1 mol salt : 5 mol H₂O, the hydrate is salt·5H₂O and is named pentahydrate.

Stoichiometry error checklist
  • Equation must be balanced before using coefficients.
  • Coefficients compare moles, not grams.
  • Do not use the excess reactant to calculate final product after finding the limiting reactant.
  • The limiting reactant is not necessarily the smallest mass or smallest number of moles.
  • Track units through every conversion.
  • Round at the end and apply sig figs.

1.1 · The Mole & Molar Mass

The mole is the bridge between a measurable laboratory mass and an unimaginably large number of microscopic particles.

Atomic mass unit (amu) vs molar mass

One atomic mass unit is defined relative to carbon-12. An individual atom's mass can be stated in amu, while a mole-sized sample is described in grams per mole.

atomic mass ≈ X amu per atom ↔ molar mass ≈ X g/mol
Why this is useful: periodic-table atomic masses give a direct conversion between grams and moles.

Add the atomic masses for all atoms in the chemical formula.

At fixed mass, n = m/M. The smaller molar mass produces more moles and more atoms.

1.2 · Isotopes, Average Atomic Mass & Mass Spectrometry

An element can contain atoms with different masses because neutron number can vary without changing proton number.

Isotope notation and particle counts
AZX
protons = Z
neutrons = A − Z

For a neutral atom, electrons = Z. For an ion, adjust electron count according to charge.

Example · ⁹⁰₃₈Sr

38 p, 52 n, 38 e if neutral.

Example · ⁴⁰K vs ⁴⁰Ar

Not isotopes: they have different atomic numbers, so they are different elements.

Example · ⁹⁰Sr vs ⁹⁴Sr

They are isotopes: same element/proton count, different neutron counts.

Why periodic-table atomic masses are decimals

The periodic-table atomic mass is a weighted average over the naturally occurring isotopes. It is not the mass number of a single atom.

average atomic mass = Σ(isotope mass × fractional abundance)
No fractional neutron: chlorine's average near 35.45 does not mean a chlorine atom contains “18.45 neutrons.”
Weighted-average examples
Neon
(20)(0.9000)+(21)(0.0199)+(22)(0.0801)=20.18 amu

The answer is close to 20 because mass-20 is overwhelmingly most abundant.

Two-isotope system
(85)(0.71)+(87)(0.29)=85.58 amu
Reverse abundance problems

For two isotopes with masses m₁ and m₂ and average M:

m₁x + m₂(1−x) = M

Solve x as the fractional abundance of isotope 1.

Mass spectrometry: how to read the graph

A mass spectrum separates isotope signals. For the level expected here:

Peak informationMeaning
horizontal positionisotopic mass / mass-to-charge position
relative peak intensityrelative abundance
Important: the tallest peak is the most abundant isotope, not automatically the heaviest isotope.

The chlorine materials show two major peaks near masses 35 and 37, with the mass-35 isotope much more abundant; the weighted average therefore lies much closer to 35.

AP-style graph reasoning
  • If peak positions stay fixed but heights change, isotope identities are unchanged but abundances differ.
  • If a sample is enriched in the heavier isotope, the average atomic mass increases.
  • The weighted average must lie between the lightest and heaviest isotope masses.
  • It must lie closer to whichever isotope is more abundant.

B · Photons, quantization, and line spectra

Planck's quantum idea says energy exchange occurs in discrete amounts. A photon carries an energy E = hν.

Excited atoms do not emit every possible wavelength. They emit a line spectrum: discrete wavelengths corresponding to allowed energy changes.

Why lines instead of a continuous rainbow? Electron energy changes are restricted to specific ΔE values. Each allowed ΔE produces a photon of one corresponding frequency.
|ΔE| = hν

Each element has a characteristic set of energy levels and therefore a characteristic emission spectrum. That is why flame colors and atomic spectra can identify elements.

C · Bohr model

Bohr proposed that the hydrogen electron occupies only specific energy levels. Absorbing the right amount of energy moves it upward; emission occurs when it returns to a lower level.

n = 1

Ground state = lowest possible energy. Excited state = electron occupies a higher-energy allowed state.

Limit: the Bohr model explains hydrogen very well but fails as a complete model for multi-electron atoms.
D · Wave-particle duality and the quantum model

Light behaves as both a wave and a stream of photons. de Broglie extended wave behavior to matter:

λ = h/(mv)

Schrödinger's wave-mechanical treatment describes electron states mathematically. The model predicts probability distributions, not fixed circular paths.

Orbit vs orbital: a Bohr orbit is a proposed fixed path. A quantum orbital is a probability distribution associated with an allowed electron state.

Heisenberg's uncertainty principle is consistent with the idea that precise classical trajectory information is not available for an electron.

1.6 · Photoelectron Spectroscopy (PES)

PES turns electron configuration from a model into something testable: electrons in different subshells require different energies to remove.

What PES measures

Energetic photons eject electrons from gaseous atoms. Measuring the energy of emitted electrons allows the binding energy of the original electrons to be inferred.

more tightly held electron → larger binding energy

Core electrons are usually closer to the nucleus and less shielded than valence electrons, so they are much more tightly bound.

Why 2s and 2p have different binding energies

2s electron density penetrates closer to the nucleus than 2p electron density. The 2s electrons experience stronger nuclear attraction on average and are generally more tightly bound.

Comparing PES across elements and ions

For electrons in comparable environments, increased nuclear charge shifts binding energies upward because electrons are held more tightly.

similar shielding + more protons → stronger attraction → higher binding energy

Isoelectronic species illustrate this cleanly: S²⁻, Ar, and Ca²⁺ all have 18 electrons, but Ca²⁺ has the most protons and holds the electron cloud most strongly.

1.7 · Periodicity & Periodic Trends

Do not memorize trend arrows alone. Nearly every trend question can be reasoned from nuclear charge, electron-shell distance, shielding, and electron-electron repulsion.

The four pieces of reasoning
Nuclear chargemore protons → stronger attraction
Distancefarther electrons → weaker attraction
Shieldingcore electrons reduce outer-electron attraction
Repulsionelectrons repel other electrons
Coulombic attraction increases with charge and decreases strongly with distance.
Effective nuclear charge and shielding

An outer electron does not feel the full nuclear charge because inner electrons repel/shield it. Across a period, proton count increases while core shielding changes relatively little, so effective nuclear attraction on valence electrons generally increases.

Across a period: electrons are added to the same broad principal shell while nuclear charge increases → electron cloud contracts.
Atomic radius
general trend: radius increases ↓ and ←

Across: stronger effective nuclear attraction pulls valence density inward.

Down: a new principal shell is occupied; greater distance and shielding dominate.

Na vs Cl

Na is larger. Both use the n=3 valence shell, but Cl has more protons and stronger effective nuclear attraction.

Li vs Cs

Cs is larger because its valence electron occupies a much higher principal level farther from the nucleus.

Ionization energy

First ionization energy is the energy required to remove one electron from a gaseous atom.

X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻
general trend: IE₁ increases ↑ and →

Across: electrons are held more tightly as effective nuclear attraction increases. Down: valence electrons are farther away and more shielded.

Successive ionization energies

For Mg, the first two electrons are valence electrons; removing a third reaches a core electron, so IE₃ is dramatically larger.

Large jump after IEₙ: often indicates n valence electrons were removed before the next removal reached a core shell.
Ionization-energy exceptions

The simple across-period trend has exceptions because subshell energies and electron pairing matter.

  • Removing an electron from a newly occupied p subshell can be easier than removing one from a filled s subshell.
  • Pairing two electrons in one orbital creates extra repulsion, sometimes lowering the energy required to remove one.
Exam strategy: if actual numerical data are supplied, use the data. Trends are general patterns, not inviolable laws.
Electronegativity

Electronegativity measures how strongly an atom attracts shared electrons in a bond.

general trend: electronegativity increases ↑ and →

Small atoms with strong effective nuclear attraction tend to pull bonding electron density strongly. Fluorine is the standard highest-electronegativity reference.

Electron affinity

Electron affinity concerns the energetic change associated with adding an electron to a gaseous atom. Its pattern is less smooth than atomic radius because electron configuration and subshell stability matter strongly.

Do not force a simplistic arrow when actual electron-affinity values are given.
Metals, nonmetals, and reactivity

Metals generally lose electrons; nonmetals generally gain/share them. Reactive metals are concentrated toward the lower-left, where electrons are relatively easy to remove. Strongly electron-attracting nonmetals occur toward the upper-right.
