Topic 1

Accuracy vs. Precision + Percent Error

High yield

Key idea: a measurement can be close to the correct value, close to otthe measurements, both, or neither. Those are two separate ideas.

Accuracy

How close a measured value is to the accepted/true value.

Memory: accurate = “aimed at the right answer.”

Precision

How close repeated measurements are to each other.

Memory: precise = “packed together.”
% error = |experimental − accepted| ÷ accepted × 100%
Why the absolute value? Percent error usually reports the size of the error, not whetthe the experimental value was above or below the accepted value.
Four possible measurement patterns
PatternWhat it meansExample if accepted = 10.00 g
accurate + preciseclustered near truth10.01, 10.00, 9.99
precise, not accurateclustered, but wrong center8.41, 8.42, 8.41
accurate on average, not precisespread out around truth8.0, 10.1, 11.9
neitherspread out and away from truth6.1, 7.7, 8.4
Worked percent-error example

A student measures the density of aluminum as 2.62 g/cm³. Accepted density = 2.70 g/cm³.

% error = |2.62 − 2.70| / 2.70 × 100 = 2.96%

Report 2.96% if the given values justify three significant figures.

Trap: the accepted value goes in the denominator, not the experimental value.
AP-style reasoning: systematic vs random error

Systematic error pushes measurements consistently in one direction (for example, a balance that always reads +0.25 g). It often harms accuracy while leaving precision high.

Random error makes measurements scatter unpredictably and often harms precision.

AP move: When a prompt describes an instrument problem, ask: does it shift every value the same way, or does it make values fluctuate?
Mini drill
  1. Accepted mass = 25.00 g; measured mass = 24.20 g. Find percent error.
  2. Three trials are 12.51, 12.50, 12.52 mL, but accepted value is 13.20 mL. Accurate, precise, both, or neither?
  3. A thermometer always reads 1.5 °C too high. Which is primarily affected: accuracy or precision?
Answers: (1) 3.20% (2) precise but inaccurate (3) accuracy; this is systematic error.
Topic 2

Significant Figures

Calculation rule

Significant figures communicate how precisely a quantity was measured. They are not decorative zeros.

RuleExampleSig figsWhy
All nonzero digits count3473every digit carries information
Leading zeros do not count0.004503zeros only locate decimal; 4,5,0 are significant
Zeros between nonzero digits count10074they are “captive”
Trailing zeros after a decimal count2.3004they show measured precision
Whole-number trailing zeros can be ambiguous1500ambiguoususe scientific notation to clarify
Multiplication and division

Round the final answer to the fewest significant figures among measured inputs.

6.24 × 2.1 = 13.104 → 13 (2 sig figs)
Trap: This is NOT about decimal places.
Addition and subtraction

Round the final answer to the least precise decimal place.

12.11 + 0.3 + 1.004 = 13.414 → 13.4

0.3 only reaches the tenths place, so the result must stop at tenths.

Trap: Do not count total sig figs for +/−.
Exact numbers and conversion factors

Counted quantities and defined conversion factors do not limit significant figures. For example, exactly 3 students or the definition 1 in = 2.54 cm are treated as exact.

The uncertainty comes from measurements, not exact definitions.
Scientific notation as a sig-fig tool

Scientific notation makes intended precision obvious:

1.5 × 10³ = 2 sig figs; 1.50 × 10³ = 3 sig figs; 1.500 × 10³ = 4 sig figs.
Mini drill
  1. How many sig figs: 0.003040?
  2. Round 7.8421 × 0.22 correctly.
  3. Round 19.876 + 2.4 correctly.
  4. Write 4500 with exactly 3 sig figs.
Answers: (1) 4 (3,0,4,0) (2) 1.7 (3) 22.3 (4) 4.50 × 10³.
Exact numbers vs. measured numbers — why some numbers do NOT limit significant figures

Significant figures only track uncertainty in measured quantities. If a number is exact, it does not add uncertainty and therefore does not limit the final number of significant figures.

Fast test: Ask, “Was this number measured, or is it known exactly by counting/definition?”
Measured → can limit sig figs.
Exact → does not limit sig figs.

Example 1: “Exactly 3 times as long”

A measured length is 2.375 cm. Another object is exactly 3 times as long.

2.375 cm × 3 = 7.125 cm

The number 3 is exact. It does not mean “1 significant figure.” The measured value 2.375 has 4 significant figures, so the final answer keeps 4 significant figures:

7.125 cm

Example 2: 4 wheels per car

There are exactly 4 wheels on each car. If 12 cars are counted:

12 cars × (4 wheels / 1 car) = 48 wheels

The 4 is exact because it comes from counting/definition. It does not force the answer to 1 significant figure.

Example 3: 2 hydrogen atoms per H₂ molecule

If there are exactly 7 H₂ molecules:

7 molecules × (2 H atoms / 1 molecule) = 14 H atoms

Both 7 and 2 are counted quantities, so they are exact.

Example 4: 1 meter = 100 centimeters

This metric relationship is defined exactly:

1 m = 100 cm

The 100 does not mean “1 significant figure.” Defined conversion factors do not limit sig figs.

Example 5: 60 seconds = 1 minute

When converting 3.42 min to seconds:

3.42 min × (60 s / 1 min) = 205.2 s

The 60 is exact, so the limiting value is 3.42, which has 3 significant figures.

205 s (3 sig figs)

Example 6: measured 3.0 vs exact 3

This distinction matters:

ExpressionWhat the “3” meansSig-fig effect
2.375 × 3 where the problem says “exactly 3 times”exact relationshipdoes not limit
2.375 × 3.0 where 3.0 is a measured quantitymeasurement with 2 sig figslimits answer to 2 sig figs
2.375 × 3.0 = 7.125 → 7.1

Example 7: 24 students split equally into 6 groups

24 students ÷ 6 groups = 4 students/group

If 24 and 6 are exact counts, they do not trigger ordinary measurement-style sig-fig rounding.

Example 8: a dozen

One dozen means exactly 12.

3 dozen eggs × 12 eggs/dozen = 36 eggs

The 12 is exact by definition.

Example 9: conversion using 1 kg = 1000 g

2.50 kg × (1000 g / 1 kg) = 2500 g

The 1000 is exact. The measured value 2.50 has 3 significant figures, so write the answer clearly as:

2.50 × 10³ g

Example 10: density calculation where every value is measured

Suppose mass = 12.4 g and volume = 3.1 mL.

density = 12.4 g ÷ 3.1 mL = 4.0 g/mL

Here both numbers are measured. 3.1 has only 2 significant figures, so the answer is limited to 2 significant figures.

Common mistake: Looking only at how many digits are written. A plain “3” can either be exact or measured depending on context. The wording of the problem tells you which.

Quick practice

  1. A ribbon is measured as 4.286 m. Another ribbon is exactly 2 times as long. What is the correctly reported length?
  2. A measured mass of 8.45 g is multiplied by a measured factor of 2.0. How many significant figures should the final answer have?
  3. Convert 6.30 min to seconds. Does the number 60 limit the answer?
  4. There are exactly 5 test tubes in each rack and exactly 8 racks. How many test tubes are there?
  5. Convert 0.450 m to cm using 100 cm = 1 m. How many significant figures should the final answer have?
Answers:
1. 8.572 m — 2 is exact; 4.286 controls the precision.
2. 2 significant figures — 2.0 is measured.
3. 378 s — 60 is exact; 6.30 has 3 sig figs.
4. 40 test tubes — both are exact counts.
5. 45.0 cm — 100 is exact; 0.450 has 3 sig figs.
Topic 3

Metric Conversions + Dimensional Analysis

Core skill

Dimensional analysis is “unit algebra.” The conversion factor must be oriented so the unwanted unit cancels.

PrefixSymbolFactor
kilok10³
centic10⁻²
millim10⁻³
microµ10⁻⁶
nanon10⁻⁹
Important rule: Use factor-label analysis first. “Moving the decimal” is only a shortcut after the conversion itself makes sense.
One-step conversion

Convert 3.75 km to m.

3.75 km × (1000 m / 1 km) = 3.75 × 10³ m

The km units cancel. The surviving unit is m.

Multi-step conversion

Convert 325 days to seconds.

325 d × (24 h/1 d) × (60 min/1 h) × (60 s/1 min) = 2.808 × 10⁷ s

If 325 is taken as 3 significant figures, report 2.81 × 10⁷ s.

Squared and cubed units

When the unit is squared or cubed, the conversion factor must also be squared or cubed.

2.00 cm² × (1 m / 100 cm)² = 2.00 × 10⁻⁴ m²
Classic trap: 1 cm = 10⁻² m, but 1 cm² = 10⁻⁴ m².
Volume relationships worth knowing
1.000 mL = 1.000 cm³
1.000 L = 1000. mL = 1.000 dm³

These are especially useful when density connects mass and volume.

Density as dimensional analysis
D = m/V

If D = 1.25 g/mL and V = 8.40 mL:

m = D×V = (1.25 g/mL)(8.40 mL) = 10.5 g

The mL cancels automatically.

Mini drill
  1. 515 m → km
  2. 2.50 L → mL
  3. 8.00 cm³ → mL
  4. 0.375 kg → mg
  5. 45.0 mi/h → m/s using 1 mi = 1609 m and 1 h = 3600 s
Answers: 0.515 km; 2.50×10³ mL; 8.00 mL; 3.75×10⁵ mg; 20.1 m/s.
Extra Practice

Unit Conversions: From Tiny to Huge

Step-by-step

Metric conversions get much easier when you stop treating them as a “move the decimal” trick. Instead, treat the units like algebra: multiply by conversion factors so the unit you do not want cancels and the unit you do want remains.

The one rule to follow every time: put the unit you want to cancel on the opposite side of the fraction. If cm is in the starting value, put cm in the denominator of the conversion factor.

Metric prefixes to know

PrefixSymbolMeaningPower of 10
kilok1,000 base units10³
hectoh100 base units10²
dekada10 base units10¹
base unitm, g, Lmeter, gram, liter10⁰
decid0.1 base unit10⁻¹
centic0.01 base unit10⁻²
millim0.001 base unit10⁻³
microµ0.000001 base unit10⁻⁶
nanon0.000000001 base unit10⁻⁹
picop0.000000000001 base unit10⁻¹²
Useful way to think about prefixes:
centi = 10⁻², milli = 10⁻³, micro = 10⁻⁶, nano = 10⁻⁹, pico = 10⁻¹².
As the unit gets smaller, you need more of those units to represent the same amount.

Example 1: 10 centimeters → kilometers

Start with the given value and convert in logical steps.

1
Convert centimeters to meters.
10 cm × (1 m / 100 cm) = 0.10 m
The cm units cancel.
2
Convert meters to kilometers.
0.10 m × (1 km / 1000 m) = 0.00010 km
The m units cancel.
Answer: 10 cm = 0.00010 km = 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ km
Why the number got smaller: a kilometer is a much larger unit than a centimeter. It takes only a tiny fraction of a kilometer to represent 10 cm.

Example 2: 10 centimeters → micrometers

This time the final unit is much smaller than a centimeter, so the numerical value becomes much larger.

10 cm × (1 m / 100 cm) × (10⁶ µm / 1 m)

Cancel cm, then cancel m:

10 × (1/100) × 10⁶ = 1.0 × 10⁵ µm
Answer: 10 cm = 100,000 µm = 1.0 × 10⁵ µm

Example 3: 10 centimeters → nanometers

10 cm × (1 m / 100 cm) × (10⁹ nm / 1 m) = 1.0 × 10⁸ nm
Answer: 10 cm = 100,000,000 nm
Important: centimeters measure length. Picograms measure mass. You cannot convert centimeters directly into picograms because they describe different physical quantities. You can only convert length → length, mass → mass, volume → volume, and so on unless another relationship such as density is provided.

Example 4: 10 grams → picograms

This is the kind of conversion where picograms make sense because both units measure mass.

1
Know what pico means:
1 pg = 10⁻¹² g
That also means:
1 g = 10¹² pg
2
Set up the factor so grams cancel:
10 g × (10¹² pg / 1 g)
3
Multiply:
10 × 10¹² = 1.0 × 10¹³ pg
Answer: 10 g = 10,000,000,000,000 pg = 1.0 × 10¹³ pg

Example 5: 3500 picograms → grams

3500 pg × (1 g / 10¹² pg) = 3.5 × 10⁻⁹ g
Answer: 3500 pg = 3.5 × 10⁻⁹ g

Example 6: 2.4 milligrams → picograms

There are two clean ways to do this. The safest way is to go through grams.

2.4 mg × (1 g / 10³ mg) × (10¹² pg / 1 g)

Cancel mg, then cancel g:

2.4 × 10⁻³ × 10¹² = 2.4 × 10⁹ pg
Answer: 2.4 mg = 2.4 × 10⁹ pg

Example 7: 0.0065 kilometers → centimeters

0.0065 km × (1000 m / 1 km) × (100 cm / 1 m)
0.0065 × 1000 × 100 = 650 cm
Answer: 0.0065 km = 650 cm

Example 8: 4.2 nanograms → milligrams

4.2 ng × (1 g / 10⁹ ng) × (10³ mg / 1 g)
4.2 × 10⁻⁹ × 10³ = 4.2 × 10⁻⁶ mg
Answer: 4.2 ng = 4.2 × 10⁻⁶ mg

Shortcut using powers of ten

Once the full dimensional-analysis method makes sense, prefix exponents can be used as a shortcut. Compare the exponent of the starting prefix with the exponent of the ending prefix.

Example: cm → km
centi = 10⁻²
kilo = 10³

The units are 5 powers of ten apart:
10⁻² / 10³ = 10⁻⁵
So:
10 cm × 10⁻⁵ = 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ km
Example: mg → pg
milli = 10⁻³
pico = 10⁻¹²

The smaller picogram unit means the numerical value becomes larger:
10⁻³ / 10⁻¹² = 10⁹
So:
2.4 mg × 10⁹ = 2.4 × 10⁹ pg

How to know whether your answer should be bigger or smaller

You are converting to...What should happen to the number?Example
a smaller unitnumber gets bigger1 g = 1000 mg
a larger unitnumber gets smaller1000 m = 1 km
This is a powerful reasonableness check. If you convert 2 meters to centimeters and get 0.02 cm, the setup is backwards because centimeters are smaller units, so you should have more of them.

One-step vs. multi-step conversions

One-step
5.2 kg × (1000 g / 1 kg) = 5200 g
Multi-step
5.2 kg × (1000 g / 1 kg) × (10¹² pg / 1 g) = 5.2 × 10¹⁵ pg

Practice: set these up before using a calculator

  1. 25.0 cm → km
  2. 0.00450 km → mm
  3. 7.2 g → pg

  5. 3.65 mg → ng
  6. 6.20 × 10⁻⁸ g → pg
  7. 4.5 µm → m
  8. 2.15 × 10⁷ nm → cm
Answers:
1. 2.50 × 10⁻⁴ km
2. 4.50 × 10³ mm
3. 7.2 × 10¹² pg

Common mistakes

1. Mixing dimensions. cm cannot become g or pg unless a separate physical relationship is given.
2. Flipping the conversion factor. Check whether the unwanted unit actually cancels.
3. Forgetting exponent signs. milli is 10⁻³; micro is 10⁻⁶; nano is 10⁻⁹; pico is 10⁻¹².
4. Guessing based on decimal movement. Write the units first. Let cancellation decide the setup.
5. Ignoring significant figures. After converting, round according to the precision of the measured starting value.
Topic 4

Proton / Neutron / Electron Calculations

Atomic basics

This is a bookkeeping problem once four definitions are secure.

Atomic number Z

= number of protons. It identifies the element.

Mass number A

= protons + neutrons for one isotope.

Neutral atom

protons = electrons.

Ion charge

charge = protons − electrons.

neutrons = A − Z
electrons = protons − charge   (use the algebraic sign)
Example: neutral isotope

For ³⁴₁₆S:

p = 16; n = 34−16 = 18; e = 16
Example: cation

For ⁵⁶₂₆Fe³⁺:

p = 26; n = 30; e = 26−(+3) = 23
Cation = positive = lost electrons.
Example: anion

For ³⁵₁₇Cl⁻:

p = 17; n = 18; e = 17−(−1) = 18
Anion = negative = gained electrons.
Mass number vs average atomic mass

Mass number is a whole-number count of protons + neutrons in a particular isotope. The decimal number on a periodic table is generally an average atomic mass across naturally occurring isotopes.

Do not subtract the decimal periodic-table mass from atomic number unless the problem explicitly tells you to approximate a mass number.
Mini drill
  1. ²¹⁰₈₂Pb: p, n, e?
  2. ²⁷₁₃Al³⁺: p, n, e?
  3. ³²₁₆S²⁻: p, n, e?
  4. An ion has 20 p and 18 e. What is its charge?
Answers: (1) 82, 128, 82 (2) 13, 14, 10 (3) 16, 16, 18 (4) 2+.
Topic 5

Naming: Binary + “Ternary” / Polyatomic Compounds

Largest memory load

The first step is classification. Do not start naming until you know what kind of compound you are looking at.

Ionic

metal + nonmetal, or compound containing a polyatomic ion.

Molecular / covalent

two nonmetals; use prefixes.

Acid

often begins with H and is aqueous; special naming rules.

Binary ionic: fixed-charge metals

Name cation first, then change monatomic anion ending to -ide.

NaCl → sodium chloride
Mg₃N₂ → magnesium nitride

No prefixes. The formula already encodes the charge balance.

Variable-charge metals and Roman numerals

For many transition/post-transition metals, determine the metal charge from the anion charge and subscripts.

CuO: O is 2− → Cu must be 2+ → copper(II) oxide
Cu₂O: O is 2− total; two Cu share +2 → each Cu is +1 → copper(I) oxide
Roman numeral = metal oxidation state/charge, NOT number of atoms.
Writing ionic formulas: charge balance

Total positive charge + total negative charge must equal zero.

Al³⁺ + O²⁻ → Al₂O₃

Two Al³⁺ give +6; three O²⁻ give −6.

Ca²⁺ + NO₃⁻ → Ca(NO₃)₂

Use parentheses when more than one copy of a polyatomic ion is needed.

Topic 6

Physical vs. Chemical Changes

Conceptual

The key question is not “did it look dramatic?” The key question is did the chemical identity/composition change?

Physical change

Same substance before and after; state, size, shape, or physical arrangement changes.

Examples: melting, boiling, freezing, crushing, dissolving sugar in water.

Chemical change

Atoms are rearranged into different substances with different compositions.

Examples: burning, rusting, acid reacting with metal, decomposition.

Physical vs chemical PROPERTIES

A physical property can be observed without changing identity: color, density, melting point, conductivity.

A chemical property describes the ability to undergo chemical change: flammability, reactivity with acid, tendency to oxidize.

Evidence of a chemical reaction — evidence, not proof by itself

Possible clues include gas formation, precipitate formation, sustained color change, temperature/light change, or new odor. But the real criterion is formation of new substances.

Boiling creates bubbles but is still physical. Bubble formation alone does not guarantee a chemical reaction.
Particle-level reasoning

On an AP-style particle diagram, a physical change preserves the same particle identities but changes spacing/arrangement. A chemical change shows old bonds broken and/or new combinations of atoms.

Mini drill
  1. Ice melts.
  2. Iron rusts.
  3. Water boils.
  4. A white solid forms when two clear solutions are mixed.
  5. A copper wire is bent.
  6. Hydrogen and oxygen react to form water.
Answers: physical, chemical, physical, chemical (new precipitate), physical, chemical.
