Before the official Unit 1 sequence, the class resources assume you can identify subatomic particles, interpret nuclear notation, understand how the atomic model developed, and read the basic organization of the periodic table.

The most important distinction: changing neutrons makes a different isotope; changing electrons makes an ion; changing protons makes a different element.
Worked example · ⁵¹₂₃V

Z = 23, so vanadium has 23 protons. A = 51, so neutrons = 51 − 23 = 28. If the symbol has no ionic charge, electrons = 23.

Worked example · ²³₁₁Na⁺

11 protons, 12 neutrons, and 10 electrons. A +1 ion has one fewer electron than protons.

2 · How the atomic model changed with evidence
DemocritusEarly proposal that matter contains tiny particles.
DaltonAtomic theory connected fixed composition and conservation of matter to atoms.
J. J. ThomsonCathode-ray evidence showed atoms contain negative particles: electrons.
MillikanOil-drop experiment measured the electron's charge.
Rutherford / gold foilRare large α-particle deflections showed positive charge and most mass are concentrated in a tiny nucleus; atoms are mostly empty space.
ChadwickEstablished the neutron as a neutral nuclear particle.
Bohr → quantum modelQuantized energy explained hydrogen spectra; wave mechanics later replaced fixed electron paths with orbitals/probability.
Gold-foil logic: Most α particles passed straight through → most of the atom is empty space. A tiny fraction deflected strongly → positive charge must be concentrated in a very small, dense region.

4 · The periodic table as an atomic map

Elements are arranged in order of increasing atomic number. A period is a horizontal row; a group is a vertical column. Repeated valence-electron patterns lead to repeated chemical behavior.

Group 1alkali metals
Group 2alkaline earth metals
Groups 3–12transition metals
Group 17halogens
Group 18noble gases

Metals are mainly left/center, commonly conduct heat/electricity and tend to form cations. Nonmetals lie mainly upper-right and commonly form anions or covalent bonds. Metalloids have intermediate properties.

5 · Elemental forms and the seven diatomic elements
H₂, N₂, O₂, F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, I₂
Only when elemental. Chlorine gas is Cl₂, but chloride inside NaCl is not “Cl₂.” The subscript belongs to the pure elemental molecule, not every appearance of the element.
1.1 · The Mole & Molar Mass

The mole is the bridge between a measurable laboratory mass and an unimaginably large number of microscopic particles.

Core idea · Counting by weighing

You can count identical or nearly identical objects without counting them one-by-one if you know their average mass. Chemistry applies the same logic to atoms.

1 dozen
12 objects
1 mole
6.022 × 10²³ entities
Nₐ = 6.022 × 10²³ mol⁻¹

An “entity” can be an atom, molecule, formula unit, or ion. Always name what is being counted.

SubstanceEntity
Neatoms
CO₂molecules
NaClformula units
Na⁺ions
Atomic mass unit (amu) vs molar mass

One atomic mass unit is defined relative to carbon-12. An individual atom's mass can be stated in amu, while a mole-sized sample is described in grams per mole.

atomic mass ≈ X amu per atom ↔ molar mass ≈ X g/mol
Why this is useful: periodic-table atomic masses give a direct conversion between grams and moles.
How to calculate molar mass

Add the atomic masses for all atoms in the chemical formula.

Example · Ca(NO₃)₂

1 Ca + 2 N + 6 O:

40.08 + 2(14.01) + 6(16.00) = 164.10 g/mol
Parentheses: a subscript outside parentheses multiplies every atom inside the parentheses.
Master conversion map

moles× Nₐ
→
particles

Going backward reverses the operation.

moles = mass / molar mass
particles = moles × 6.022 × 10²³
Worked examples
A · 23.0 g CO₂ → molecules
23.0 g × (1 mol/44.01 g) = 0.5226 mol
0.5226 mol × 6.022×10²³ = 3.15×10²³ molecules
B · 0.250 mol SO₃ → oxygen atoms
0.250 mol SO₃ × 6.022×10²³ molecules/mol × 3 O atoms/molecule
4.52 × 10²³ O atoms
C · Equal masses: which sample contains more atoms?

At fixed mass, n = m/M. The smaller molar mass produces more moles and more atoms.

How to check whether a mole answer makes sense
  • If the sample mass is much smaller than one molar mass, the sample should contain less than 1 mol.
  • A fraction of a mole can still contain an enormous number of particles.
  • Units should cancel exactly.
  • A formula subscript creates an additional particle ratio when counting a particular atom/ion.
Practice
  1. How many mol Ca are in 140. g?
  2. How many formula units are in 0.522 g CuBr₂?
  3. How many chloride ions are in 0.300 mol CaCl₂?
Answers
  1. ≈3.49 mol
  2. ≈1.41×10²¹ formula units
  3. ≈3.61×10²³ Cl⁻ ions
1.2 · Isotopes, Average Atomic Mass & Mass Spectrometry

An element can contain atoms with different masses because neutron number can vary without changing proton number.

Isotope notation and particle counts
AZX
protons = Z
neutrons = A − Z

For a neutral atom, electrons = Z. For an ion, adjust electron count according to charge.

Example · ⁹⁰₃₈Sr

38 p, 52 n, 38 e if neutral.

Example · ⁴⁰K vs ⁴⁰Ar

Not isotopes: they have different atomic numbers, so they are different elements.

Example · ⁹⁰Sr vs ⁹⁴Sr

They are isotopes: same element/proton count, different neutron counts.

Why periodic-table atomic masses are decimals

The periodic-table atomic mass is a weighted average over the naturally occurring isotopes. It is not the mass number of a single atom.

average atomic mass = Σ(isotope mass × fractional abundance)
No fractional neutron: chlorine's average near 35.45 does not mean a chlorine atom contains “18.45 neutrons.”
Weighted-average examples
Neon
(20)(0.9000)+(21)(0.0199)+(22)(0.0801)=20.18 amu

The answer is close to 20 because mass-20 is overwhelmingly most abundant.

Two-isotope system
(85)(0.71)+(87)(0.29)=85.58 amu
Reverse abundance problems

For two isotopes with masses m₁ and m₂ and average M:

m₁x + m₂(1−x) = M

Solve x as the fractional abundance of isotope 1.

Mass spectrometry: how to read the graph

A mass spectrum separates isotope signals. For the level expected here:

Peak informationMeaning
horizontal positionisotopic mass / mass-to-charge position
relative peak intensityrelative abundance
Important: the tallest peak is the most abundant isotope, not automatically the heaviest isotope.

The chlorine materials show two major peaks near masses 35 and 37, with the mass-35 isotope much more abundant; the weighted average therefore lies much closer to 35.

AP-style graph reasoning
  • If peak positions stay fixed but heights change, isotope identities are unchanged but abundances differ.
  • If a sample is enriched in the heavier isotope, the average atomic mass increases.
  • The weighted average must lie between the lightest and heaviest isotope masses.
  • It must lie closer to whichever isotope is more abundant.
1.3 · Percent Composition, Empirical Formula & Molecular Formula

This topic reverses the mole idea: instead of using a known formula to count atoms, you use measured masses to discover the atom ratio in an unknown compound.

Percent composition from a known formula
% element = (mass contributed by that element in 1 mol compound / molar mass compound) ×100
Example · glucose, C₆H₁₂O₆
C contribution = 6(12.01)=72.06 g
M ≈180.16 g/mol
%C≈72.06/180.16×100≈40.0%
Check: percentages of all elements should total about 100% (small discrepancy may come from rounding).
Empirical vs molecular formula

Empirical

Simplest whole-number ratio.

CH₂O

Molecular

Actual atom counts in one molecule.

C₆H₁₂O₆

C₆H₆, C₂H₂, and C₈H₈ can all have empirical formula CH even though they are different molecular substances.

Why composition alone cannot always give the molecular formula: a 1:2:1 C:H:O ratio could correspond to CH₂O, C₂H₄O₂, C₆H₁₂O₆, and more. Molar mass is needed to distinguish them.

For a C/H/O compound burned completely:

Reality checks and common errors
  • Empirical formula must have the simplest whole-number ratio.
  • Molecular formula must be an integer multiple of the empirical formula.
  • The molecular molar mass cannot be smaller than the empirical-formula mass.
  • If n is nowhere near a whole number, recheck arithmetic, molar masses, and experimental data.
  • Percent values should total approximately 100%.

Fixed composition vs variable composition

A pure compound has a definite formula. Every water molecule contains two H atoms per O atom, so every pure water sample has the same elemental mass ratio.

If three “NaCl” samples have different chlorine percentages: they cannot all be pure samples of exactly the same compound under identical accurate analysis. At least one is impure, misidentified, or measured inaccurately.

1.5 · Electronic Structure

This is a chain of evidence: light behaves in quantized ways → atoms absorb/emit only specific energies → electrons occupy allowed energy states → the quantum model describes those states as orbitals.

Example · 650 nm light
650 nm = 6.50×10⁻⁷ m
ν = (2.998×10⁸)/(6.50×10⁻⁷)=4.61×10¹⁴ s⁻¹
B · Photons, quantization, and line spectra

Planck's quantum idea says energy exchange occurs in discrete amounts. A photon carries an energy E = hν.

Excited atoms do not emit every possible wavelength. They emit a line spectrum: discrete wavelengths corresponding to allowed energy changes.

Why lines instead of a continuous rainbow? Electron energy changes are restricted to specific ΔE values. Each allowed ΔE produces a photon of one corresponding frequency.
|ΔE| = hν

Each element has a characteristic set of energy levels and therefore a characteristic emission spectrum. That is why flame colors and atomic spectra can identify elements.

C · Bohr model

Bohr proposed that the hydrogen electron occupies only specific energy levels. Absorbing the right amount of energy moves it upward; emission occurs when it returns to a lower level.

n = 4
n = 3
n = 2
n = 1

Ground state = lowest possible energy. Excited state = electron occupies a higher-energy allowed state.

Limit: the Bohr model explains hydrogen very well but fails as a complete model for multi-electron atoms.
D · Wave-particle duality and the quantum model

Light behaves as both a wave and a stream of photons. de Broglie extended wave behavior to matter:

λ = h/(mv)

Schrödinger's wave-mechanical treatment describes electron states mathematically. The model predicts probability distributions, not fixed circular paths.

Orbit vs orbital: a Bohr orbit is a proposed fixed path. A quantum orbital is a probability distribution associated with an allowed electron state.

Heisenberg's uncertainty principle is consistent with the idea that precise classical trajectory information is not available for an electron.

1.6 · Photoelectron Spectroscopy (PES)

PES turns electron configuration from a model into something testable: electrons in different subshells require different energies to remove.

What PES measures

Energetic photons eject electrons from gaseous atoms. Measuring the energy of emitted electrons allows the binding energy of the original electrons to be inferred.

more tightly held electron → larger binding energy

Core electrons are usually closer to the nucleus and less shielded than valence electrons, so they are much more tightly bound.

How to read a PES spectrum
FeatureMeaning
peak positionbinding energy of an electron group/subshell
peak height or arearelative number of electrons represented
high binding energymore strongly held / more core-like
low binding energymore easily removed / more valence-like
Read the axis direction. The class PES materials can plot larger binding energy to the left and may use a nonlinear axis.
Worksheet example · carbon

The worksheet shows three equal-sized peaks, which correspond to 1s², 2s², and 2p².

2+2+2 = 6 electrons → carbon

The highest-binding-energy peak is 1s because those electrons are closest to the nucleus.

Worksheet example · oxygen
1s² 2s² 2p⁴

Peak electron counts are 2, 2, and 4 → 8 total electrons and 6 valence electrons.

Why 2s and 2p have different binding energies

2s electron density penetrates closer to the nucleus than 2p electron density. The 2s electrons experience stronger nuclear attraction on average and are generally more tightly bound.

Comparing PES across elements and ions

For electrons in comparable environments, increased nuclear charge shifts binding energies upward because electrons are held more tightly.

similar shielding + more protons → stronger attraction → higher binding energy

Isoelectronic species illustrate this cleanly: S²⁻, Ar, and Ca²⁺ all have 18 electrons, but Ca²⁺ has the most protons and holds the electron cloud most strongly.

1.7 · Periodicity & Periodic Trends

Do not memorize trend arrows alone. Nearly every trend question can be reasoned from nuclear charge, electron-shell distance, shielding, and electron-electron repulsion.

The four pieces of reasoning
Nuclear chargemore protons → stronger attraction
Distancefarther electrons → weaker attraction
Shieldingcore electrons reduce outer-electron attraction
Repulsionelectrons repel other electrons
Coulombic attraction increases with charge and decreases strongly with distance.
Effective nuclear charge and shielding

An outer electron does not feel the full nuclear charge because inner electrons repel/shield it. Across a period, proton count increases while core shielding changes relatively little, so effective nuclear attraction on valence electrons generally increases.

Across a period: electrons are added to the same broad principal shell while nuclear charge increases → electron cloud contracts.
Atomic radius
general trend: radius increases ↓ and ←

Across: stronger effective nuclear attraction pulls valence density inward.

Down: a new principal shell is occupied; greater distance and shielding dominate.

Na vs Cl

Na is larger. Both use the n=3 valence shell, but Cl has more protons and stronger effective nuclear attraction.

Li vs Cs

Cs is larger because its valence electron occupies a much higher principal level farther from the nucleus.

Ionization energy

First ionization energy is the energy required to remove one electron from a gaseous atom.

X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻
general trend: IE₁ increases ↑ and →

Across: electrons are held more tightly as effective nuclear attraction increases. Down: valence electrons are farther away and more shielded.

Successive ionization energies

For Mg, the first two electrons are valence electrons; removing a third reaches a core electron, so IE₃ is dramatically larger.

Large jump after IEₙ: often indicates n valence electrons were removed before the next removal reached a core shell.
Ionization-energy exceptions

The simple across-period trend has exceptions because subshell energies and electron pairing matter.

  • Removing an electron from a newly occupied p subshell can be easier than removing one from a filled s subshell.
  • Pairing two electrons in one orbital creates extra repulsion, sometimes lowering the energy required to remove one.
Exam strategy: if actual numerical data are supplied, use the data. Trends are general patterns, not inviolable laws.
Electronegativity

Electronegativity measures how strongly an atom attracts shared electrons in a bond.

general trend: electronegativity increases ↑ and →

Small atoms with strong effective nuclear attraction tend to pull bonding electron density strongly. Fluorine is the standard highest-electronegativity reference.

Electron affinity

Electron affinity concerns the energetic change associated with adding an electron to a gaseous atom. Its pattern is less smooth than atomic radius because electron configuration and subshell stability matter strongly.

Do not force a simplistic arrow when actual electron-affinity values are given.
Metals, nonmetals, and reactivity

Metals generally lose electrons; nonmetals generally gain/share them. Reactive metals are concentrated toward the lower-left, where electrons are relatively easy to remove. Strongly electron-attracting nonmetals occur toward the upper-right.

AP-quality written explanations
Weak
“Cl is smaller because it is farther right.”
Strong
“Na and Cl have valence electrons in the same principal shell, but Cl has more protons with similar core shielding, so its greater effective nuclear attraction pulls the valence electron cloud closer.”
1.8 · Formation of Ions, Electron Configurations & Ionic Radius

Ions form when electrons are gained or lost. The nucleus remains the same element.

Cations vs anions

Cation

loses electrons → positive

Na → Na⁺ + e⁻

Anion

gains electrons → negative

Cl + e⁻ → Cl⁻
Ions never form by changing proton number in ordinary chemistry.
Main-group ion charges and noble-gas configurations

Main-group metals commonly lose enough valence electrons to reach the previous noble-gas configuration; nonmetals commonly gain enough to reach the next noble gas.

GroupTypical simple ion
1+1
2+2

Na: [Ne]3s¹ → Na⁺: [Ne]

Transition-metal ions: remove the outermost n first

Although 4s fills before 3d in neutral first-row transition atoms, electrons are removed from the highest principal level first when forming cations.

Co: [Ar]4s²3d⁷ → Co²⁺: [Ar]3d⁷
Sn: [Kr]5s²4d¹⁰5p² → Sn²⁺: [Kr]5s²4d¹⁰
Sn⁴⁺: [Kr]4d¹⁰
Why cations are smaller and anions are larger

Cations: electron loss reduces electron-electron repulsion and may remove the entire highest occupied shell. The remaining electrons experience stronger net attraction.

Anions: added electrons increase electron-electron repulsion while nuclear charge stays the same.

cation radius < neutral atom < anion radius
Isoelectronic series

Isoelectronic species have the same number of electrons. With electron count fixed, compare proton count.

more protons → stronger attraction → smaller radius
O²⁻ > F⁻ > Ne > Na⁺ > Mg²⁺ > Al³⁺

All six species have 10 electrons, but nuclear charge rises from O to Al.

Connecting ion size to ionic solids

Ionic compounds consist of extended arrays of cations and anions, not isolated molecular units. Electrostatic attraction between opposite charges contributes to high melting points. The written formula is the simplest neutral ratio.

Ca²⁺ + O²⁻ → CaO
2Al³⁺ + 3O²⁻ → Al₂O₃
